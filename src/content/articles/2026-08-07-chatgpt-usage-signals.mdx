## TL;DR

OpenAI's updated Signals dataset shows people use ChatGPT differently at work: they are more than twice as likely to ask it to produce or complete something than they are outside work. Multimedia is the fastest-growing use category, and the share of messages from people over 35 rose in nearly every measured country.

## What's New

OpenAI published country-level statistics for how individual ChatGPT accounts are used. The update says work conversations favor "doing" tasks such as editing, coding, and analysis, while conversations outside work more often seek information or clarification.

Multimedia generation, analysis, and retrieval reached 7.8% of messages globally. OpenAI also reports that people over 35 account for a 5% larger share of messages than they did a year earlier, based only on accounts with self-reported ages.

These are sampled estimates, not a census of every conversation. The methodology uses 300,000 consumer ChatGPT messages per month from July 2024 through June 2026, excludes enterprise messages and Codex, and relies on automated classifiers applied after personal information is removed. OpenAI publishes the aggregated data with differential-privacy noise and a CC BY 4.0 license.

## Why It Matters

The finding offers a useful test for your own AI habit. Asking for explanations can help you learn, but work usually advances when the conversation ends with something inspectable: a revised email, a comparison table, a checklist, or a calculation with stated assumptions.

That does not mean handing over judgment. A generated output becomes useful only after you check it against the original material and decide what to keep. The study is also OpenAI's analysis of its own consumer product, so it describes ChatGPT behavior rather than all AI use.

<PracticalGuide timeToTry="10 min" prerequisites={["Five recent non-sensitive AI prompts, or five sample tasks"]}>

1. Write five recent non-sensitive prompts on a blank page. If you do not have five, use these categories: explain, compare, draft, check, and plan.
2. Mark each prompt A if it mainly asks for information, or D if it asks for a concrete output you can inspect. Do not paste private chat history into another service.
3. Choose one A prompt that relates to a real task. Rewrite it as a D prompt with an output and a check, for example: "Turn this public event description into a six-item packing checklist. Cite the line that supports each unusual item."
4. Run the rewritten prompt with non-sensitive material. Reject any item that lacks support or cannot be checked against the source.
5. Compare the result with the original question. Keep the new format only if it saved a real step without hiding uncertainty.
6. Record one reusable pattern for future work: desired output, source material, limit, and verification step. Stop before using the pattern with confidential or regulated data unless your account and workplace policy allow it.
