## TL;DR

OpenAI published national-security principles for government AI partnerships. The practical takeaway is a checklist: human judgment, democratic accountability, no mass domestic surveillance, no autonomous weapons direction, and no high-stakes automated decisions.

## What's New

OpenAI says governments are beginning to use frontier AI in sensitive work, including national security. The company published principles meant to explain how it approaches those partnerships.

The post says OpenAI is expanding allied cyber and biosecurity partnerships while keeping restrictions from its Department of War agreement. Those restrictions cover mass domestic surveillance, autonomous weapons direction, and high-stakes automated decisions.

OpenAI also says the hardest questions about AI in government should be handled through democratic and legislative processes, not by companies alone.

## Why It Matters

Most people will never negotiate a government AI contract. But the same questions show up in schools, companies, hospitals, and local agencies: who approves use, who is monitored, and when is a human required?

This is useful because it gives readers concrete policy language to look for. A vague "responsible AI" statement is not enough if it never says what the system must not do.

<PracticalGuide timeToTry="20 min" prerequisites={["A public-sector, vendor, school, or workplace AI policy to review"]}>

1. Open OpenAI's principles and write down the three red-line areas it names.
2. Compare those red lines with any AI policy you use at work, school, or in a local organization.
3. Look for missing language on human approval, surveillance, autonomous action, and audit logs.
4. Ask your vendor or IT owner where model use is logged and who can approve sensitive deployments.
5. If the policy leaves high-stakes decisions to automation alone, flag it for legal, compliance, or leadership review.
